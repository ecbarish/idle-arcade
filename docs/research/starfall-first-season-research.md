Onboarding and Systems Design for Starfall Guild: A Comprehensive AnalysisThe development of a single-player adventuring-guild management simulation necessitates a delicate balance between strategic roster manipulation and an emotionally resonant narrative framework. The core fantasy of the proposed title requires the player to nurture a cadre of adventurers, cultivate their skills, and observe their autonomous expeditions, all while avoiding the sterile, mathematically exhaustive aesthetics of a spreadsheet. The simulation must evoke the liveliness of a bustling guild hall while maintaining the rigid, readable constraints of a management interface. This report investigates the ontological and mechanical systems required to onboard players into this ecosystem, prioritizing immediate comprehension, emotional attachment, and long-term retention.It must be explicitly stated that the current browser implementation of the game has not been directly inspected or played during the compilation of this analysis. The investigation relies on the provided documentation regarding existing features, which include seven classes, party combinations, recruitment mechanisms, shops, facilities, relic choices, earned staff, regional progression, Renown systems, season resets, and the "Hall of Legends" retention system. This report strictly separates these existing features from new proposals designed to validate the core vertical slice. Furthermore, parked features such as narrative careers, specializations, simultaneous expeditions, rival guilds, and richer town interactions are acknowledged but purposefully excluded from the immediate onboarding and validation recommendations.Managing a Roster vs. Controlling a Hero: Comparative Systems AnalysisTeaching the player the distinction between macro-management (overseeing a guild) and micro-management (controlling an avatar) represents the fundamental hurdle of the onboarding process. If the player approaches the simulation expecting direct tactical control over individual swings of a sword, they will experience profound frustration when the autonomous systems resolve combat. Conversely, if the simulation is entirely detached, the player will feel relegated to the role of a passive spectator, severely diminishing engagement. The design must establish the player as a coach, quartermaster, or guildmaster: responsible for preparation, formation, and economic support, but strictly sidelined during the chaotic execution of the expedition.To understand how to successfully frame this dynamic, it is necessary to examine established titles that navigate similar mechanical spaces, isolating the specific design decisions that succeed or fail within their respective contexts.Evaluating Comparative Management ParadigmsThe following table synthesizes the core onboarding mechanics, tonal alignment, and scope mismatches of relevant comparative titles, providing a foundational understanding of the genre's landscape.TitleOnboarding FocusRoster Management MechanicTone and Scope Alignment with Proposed GameFailure Modes and MismatchesDungeon VillageTown building and passive hero attraction.Heroes operate entirely autonomously, leveling up and utilizing town facilities based on spatial proximity and player-dictated events.High tonal alignment. The game successfully simulates a heroic hamlet where warriors autonomously fight monsters outside the town gates and return to utilize local facilities. The onboarding is deceptively simple, drawing the player in with colorful, pixel-art aesthetics and a satisfying loop of municipal expansion. However, the mechanical underpinnings suffer from severe flaws that the proposed simulation must actively circumvent.Critiques of the game frequently highlight the abysmal adventurer artificial intelligence. Units will autonomously engage enemies one by one, feeding themselves into enemy mobs and dying repeatedly upon revival because they refuse to group up or retreat. Furthermore, the monster scaling system bases enemy strength on the highest-level adventurer present in the town. This effectively guarantees the immediate and repeated death of new, lower-level recruits, preventing them from gaining the experience necessary to survive. Finally, the late-game scaling is heavily criticized; character base stats bloat to such an astronomical extent that the player's management decisions regarding equipment and class changes become completely irrelevant.The proposed simulation must avoid this mathematical inflation and AI bankruptcy. If the player's role is to prepare the party, that preparation—specifically the existing features of class synergies, party combinations, and relic choices—must remain the primary determinant of success, rather than raw, unmitigated level grinding. The tone of Dungeon Village is entirely appropriate, but its scope eventually collapses into chaotic non-interactivity. The lesson here is that autonomous actors must be governed by strict, logical behaviors (such as forming a cohesive party before leaving the guild) rather than wandering aimlessly.The Psychological Roster and the StagecoachAt the opposite end of the tonal spectrum lies Red Hook Studios' Darkest Dungeon. While its oppressive, gothic horror atmosphere is entirely mismatched for an uplifting fantasy guild, its roster management mechanics are widely considered best-in-class within the industry. The game defines the central "Hamlet" itself as a persistent RPG character, establishing it as the unwavering base of operations regardless of how many heroes perish. The developers explicitly designed the affliction and stress systems to cast the player in the role of a team manager, squad leader, or hockey coach, forcing them to balance human frailty against objective completion.The critical lesson from this title is its use of the "Stagecoach" to ensure continuous progression and prevent failure cascades. New heroes arrive randomly in the hamlet every week. This means the player is never soft-locked out of the game; even if their entire existing roster is wiped out and their treasury is empty, fresh recruits are always available to launch a desperate expedition. Furthermore, the game mechanically encourages a rotating roster by requiring traumatized heroes to spend weeks recovering in town facilities, forcing the player to utilize their secondary squad.For the proposed simulation, a similar, though less punitive, influx mechanism must exist. It should not exist to replace traumatized cannon fodder, but rather to introduce new personalities and class combinations that the player wishes to nurture. The onboarding must teach the player that resting heroes is not a penalty or a delay tactic, but a necessary rhythm that opens the door for new recruits to prove their worth.Procedural Attachment and LegacyThe title Wildermyth excels at forging profound emotional connections between the player and procedurally generated characters. Developer Nate Austin's approach avoids the pitfall of generating massive, irrelevant biographies that players simply skip reading. Instead, the game tracks specific knowledge, memories, physical maiming, and relationships dynamically over the course of a campaign. Characters interpret events based on their underlying personality traits, forming opinions that dictate their future interactions and narrative arcs.In a management game where the player cannot directly control the characters during an expedition, these procedural reactions are vital for player investment. When an adventurer returns from the Whispering Woods, they should not merely bring back a numeric value of gold; they should bring back a memory of the event, filtered through their class or hidden traits. The game utilizes a legacy system where successful heroes can be saved and recruited in future campaigns, preserving the player's emotional investment across multiple playthroughs. This directly parallels the existing "Hall of Legends" feature in the proposed simulation, validating it as a highly effective mechanism for long-term retention.The Readable Auto-BattlerTo bridge the gap between deep management and passive observation, the simulation must look to modern asynchronous auto-battlers, specifically titles like Backpack Battles. This game thrives on inventory management and spatial buildcraft, where the actual combat is resolved entirely automatically. The genius of the system lies in its extreme readability. Items trigger based on explicit cooldowns and stamina constraints, and the user interface makes it immediately clear exactly why a specific build succeeded or failed.For the expedition phase of the proposed simulation, observing combat must be an exercise in validation, not confusion. The player watches the autonomous battle to see if their management decisions were correct. Did the existing Cleric class trigger their healing spell in time to save the Swordsman? Did the chosen relic provide enough block to survive the boss's attack? The user interface must highlight these pivotal interactions, deliberately slowing down or emphasizing critical moments, rather than obscuring them behind a flurry of generic particle effects and overlapping damage numbers. The combat must act as a transparent diagnostic tool for the management phase.The First Expedition: Orchestrating the Opening SequenceThe primary directive of the first twenty minutes of gameplay is to definitively separate the act of observing from the act of meaningful intervention, thereby preventing cognitive overload. Presenting the entirety of the guild's dashboard, multiple currencies, the Hall of Legends, and complex regional maps immediately will paralyze the player. The onboarding must be ruthlessly curated, revealing systems only exactly when they become contextually necessary.Introducing the Anchor and the First DecisionThe simulation commences with a single, existing character: Ren, the Swordsman. The player is deliberately not given the choice to create Ren or customize his stats; he serves as a fixed, reliable anchor point to ground the simulation's early mechanics. The initial user interface should display only Ren's portrait, a simplified health bar, and his primary role icon (e.g., a shield denoting a frontline combatant). All other mathematical statistics, such as critical hit chance, elemental resistances, or speed, must remain hidden at this stage to avoid spreadsheet intimidation.The player's first genuine interaction is the initial recruitment decision. A narrative prompt indicates that Ren cannot survive the nearby introductory region alone. Two candidates are presented from the existing roster of seven classes, carefully selected to teach specific synergies. For example, presenting a Cleric (a dedicated healer) alongside an Archer (a ranged damage dealer). This choice immediately forces the player to consider party dynamics.Selecting the Cleric teaches the concept of attrition, sustain, and outlasting the opponent. Selecting the Archer teaches the concept of burst damage, target prioritization, and eliminating threats before they can deal damage. Once the selection is made, the interface explicitly links the two units, displaying a visual "Party Formation" grid. Ren is locked into the front line, and the new recruit is placed in the back line. By confirming this formation, the player physically interacts with the concept of tactical positioning without needing to issue commands during combat.The Preparation Phase and Relic AssignmentBefore the newly formed party departs on their first expedition, the player must make a single preparation choice. Providing a massive shop inventory with dozens of minor stat upgrades is a common onboarding error. Instead, the player is given a choice between two "Guild Relics," an existing feature in the game's architecture.These relics must offer easily understandable, binary benefits. For example, "The Vanguard Shield" (grants Ren a guaranteed block against the first attack) or "The Swift Boots" (allows the recruit to act first in every encounter). By requiring the player to physically drag and drop this relic onto a specific character's portrait, the simulation reinforces the tactile nature of management. The player internalizes that they are empowering the autonomous agents through direct, pre-expedition intervention.Readable Outcomes and Useful Next ActionsThe expedition begins, and the screen transitions to the observation view. The player has zero input during this phase. To ensure the battle is readable, the simulation must control its pacing. When Ren strikes, a clear damage number and a weighty sound effect occur. When the enemy strikes back, Ren's health bar drops visibly, perhaps flashing red to draw the eye. If the Cleric heals, a distinct, bright green aura explicitly flows from the Cleric to Ren, accompanied by a restorative audio cue. The battle must be brief—no longer than thirty to forty-five seconds—to maintain a brisk gameplay loop.Upon victory, the expedition ends, and the "Return Report" is generated. Crucially, the player does not merely receive a sterile spreadsheet listing gold and experience points. The report must dynamically highlight the specific intervention the player made. For example, a highlighted text box reading: "The Vanguard Shield absorbed 15 damage, ensuring Ren's survival." This explicitly validates the player's earlier management decision.The required next action is the immediate application of the expedition's reward. The player is guided to use the newly recovered gold to unlock or utilize the first town facility—a Tavern or resting area. This sequence perfectly encapsulates the intended core loop: Recruit and Prepare, Observe and Validate, Return and Reward, Recover and Expand.Information Gating: What to HideDuring this initial loop, several existing features must be strictly hidden. The regional map should be locked to the single introductory area. The Renown system and season resets must not be mentioned. Staff delegation, rival guilds, and the Hall of Legends are irrelevant until the player has established a functional, baseline understanding of how a single party survives a single encounter. Exposing the prestige mechanics before the player understands basic combat resolution will result in total comprehension failure.Fostering Member Attachment Without Spreadsheet BloatA lively, engaging guild requires members who feel distinct and memorable. However, the development resources of a small team do not permit the creation of endless, hand-authored, branching biographies for every procedural recruit that walks through the door. The solution lies in a modest, authored system that leverages player apophenia—the human psychological tendency to perceive meaningful connections and narratives in random data.The Contextual Memory SystemDrawing heavily from the Wildermyth procedural narrative structure, the simulation should record specific, highly contextual events that occur during the autonomous expeditions. Instead of a static, pre-written biography, each character possesses a "Memory Log" containing a limited number of slots (e.g., three to five). These slots are populated dynamically by localized triggers within the combat engine.For example, if Ren survives a battle with exactly one hit point remaining, the engine triggers a specific memory generation. The post-expedition report will state: "Ren narrowly escaped death at the hands of a Goblin. He now fights with desperate ferocity." This generates a permanent, readable trait that provides a minor mechanical buff, such as a five percent increase to damage when health falls below twenty percent.These micro-memories serve a vital dual purpose. First, they create a narrative history that is entirely unique to the player's specific playthrough, fostering deep attachment because the player feels they have witnessed the character's growth firsthand. Second, they provide subtle, specialized mechanical niches that differentiate two characters of the exact same class. A player will care about their specific Swordsman because that Swordsman has a documented history of surviving against the odds, rather than just being a generic sprite with slightly higher base stats.Balancing Rare Recruits Against Beloved VeteransA prevalent and damaging failure mode in gacha games and roster-management simulators is the introduction of rare, mechanically superior units that immediately render the player's initial, beloved units obsolete. If a "Legendary Paladin" arrives at the guild with stats double that of the starting characters, the player will feel foolish for having invested time and emotion into Ren. This destroys the cozy fantasy of nurturing a complementary party.To circumvent this, rarity within the recruitment system must dictate complexity and specialization, never absolute, raw power. A common Swordsman like Ren should possess a highly reliable, straightforward skill set: he taunts enemies and strikes with consistent physical damage. He is the reliable anchor of any formation. A rare recruit, conversely, might possess a highly complex, conditional mechanic. For example, a rare "Blood Mage" might deal massive area-of-effect damage, but only if the enemy is already afflicted by a specific status effect, and casting the spell drains the Mage's own health.Furthermore, the simulation must actively reward "Tenure." The longer a character remains active within the guild, the more they contribute to a passive, invisible "Guild Cohesion" aura. Replacing a veteran Swordsman who has survived ten expeditions with a rookie Legendary Paladin will result in a temporary, noticeable drop in overall party synergy. The new recruit might miss attacks more frequently or fail to trigger combo abilities until they have completed several runs alongside the established members. This forces the player to weigh the raw potential of a new recruit against the seasoned, reliable teamwork of their veterans, ensuring that common members are never casually discarded.Economy Clarity: Teaching Spending and TradeoffsThe economy of the guild must be ruthlessly simplified to prevent the player from viewing their role as an accounting exercise. Utilizing multiple, interlocking currencies—such as gold, gems, premium crystals, wood, stone, and influence—violates the mandate for a lively, readable simulation. The economy should be strictly restricted to two primary metrics: Gold and Renown, both of which are existing features in the current implementation.Delineating the Economic PillarsThe onboarding process must clearly define the distinct purposes of the two currencies to prevent resource hoarding or accidental bankruptcy.Gold (The Ephemeral Resource): Gold is looted directly from successful expeditions. It is used entirely for short-term survival and immediate cycle upgrades. Gold pays for character recovery (such as resting in the Tavern or visiting a healer), temporary expedition supplies, and immediate gear upgrades at the shop. The player should be encouraged to spend their Gold frequently, as it represents the day-to-day operating budget of the guild.Renown (The Persistent Resource): Renown is earned exclusively by completing major milestones, defeating regional bosses, or leveling up the guild's overall standing. Renown is the macroeconomic currency, used for permanent facility construction, expanding the maximum roster size, and unlocking new regions.Visibly Altering the Expedition ExperienceThe onboarding must rigorously teach the player that spending money results in tangible, visible changes to outcomes. If economic decisions are relegated entirely to abstract numbers on a dashboard, the player will lose interest. When the player purchases a weapon upgrade for Ren using their hard-earned Gold, the change must be more than numeric.If Ren's weapon upgrades from a basic "Iron Sword" to a "Steel Broadsword," the 2D sprite or 3D model utilized during the expedition phase must physically change to reflect the new equipment. Furthermore, the user interface during the subsequent battle should feature a "Delta Indicator." This could be a small, upward-pointing green arrow appearing next to Ren's damage numbers, explicitly signaling to the player that the damage is demonstrably higher because of the recent purchase. This immediate, visual feedback loop validates the player's management choices and makes the act of spending highly satisfying.Distinguishing Survival from InfrastructurePlayers, particularly novices to the management genre, often struggle to balance the immediate need to maintain their current roster with the long-term desire to upgrade their overall facilities. The user interface must visually segregate these distinct choices to prevent accidental failure cascades.The main "Guild Dashboard" should feature a clearly demarcated "Recovery & Upkeep" tab separate from an "Infrastructure" tab. If a player attempts to spend the entirety of their Gold on a new facility while their active adventurers are sitting at critical health levels, a non-punitive, diagetic warning should appear. This warning, perhaps delivered by an NPC staff member, would state: "Guildmaster, your primary party is severely wounded. Proceeding to the next expedition without utilizing the Tavern funds may result in catastrophic defeat." This teaches the player to prioritize short-term survival over long-term greed without resorting to punishing them with an immediate game over.Seasons, Carryover, and Prestige ArchitectureThe implementation of a "Season Reset" is an inherently dangerous design decision in a simulation predicated on deep emotional attachment and the cozy fantasy of nurturing characters. If a player spends dozens of hours carefully cultivating Ren, optimizing his relics, and watching him build a history of memories, only to have him and the entire guild permanently deleted when a numeric prestige loop triggers, the resulting psychological whiplash will likely cause immediate player churn. A harsh, numeric prestige loop must never be confused with a narrative season.The Narrative Season vs. The Numeric LoopTo understand the correct approach, it is useful to examine games that successfully integrate repetitive loops into their narrative. Supergiant Games' Hades famously utilizes narrative continuity to "take the sting out of failure". In Hades, death and the subsequent reset to the starting hub are not punitive erasures of progress, but necessary narrative beats that advance character dialogue and relationships. Every reset provides a new opportunity to interact with the world.Similarly, Cellar Door Games' Rogue Legacy utilizes an inheritance system where progress slows, and players perform a "Dark Rebirth" (or die, in the first game's context), resetting the run in exchange for permanent bonuses and the continuation of the family line. This core loop creates a strong sense of momentum and long-term mastery, rather than loss.A Season in the proposed simulation should represent the culmination of a major, narrative-driven campaign against a specific regional threat. When the season ends, the timeline advances. The threat is neutralized, and the region resets with a new ecological or narrative complication (e.g., the Whispering Woods, previously cleared of goblins, are now corrupted by a winter blight). The reset is justified by the passage of time.The Hall of Legends: Preserving InvestmentWhen a season resets, the player's economic progression—specifically their accumulated Gold and low-level facilities—may reset to simulate the beginning of a new administrative cycle and to maintain a balanced difficulty curve. However, the characters themselves must not be casually discarded. This is where the existing "Hall of Legends" feature becomes the paramount mechanism for player retention.At the conclusion of a season, the player is presented with a monumental, celebratory interface. They are required to select a core cadre of their favorite adventurers to enshrine in the Hall of Legends. These chosen characters retain their names, their physical customization, their established relationship bonds, and their accumulated "Memory Logs." Their numeric levels, however, are reset to level one. This mechanical reset is justified narratively: a period of extended peace has caused their combat skills to rust, or the new seasonal threat requires them to learn entirely new techniques.This approach ensures that the player's profound emotional investment is completely preserved. The player begins the new season not with a roster of procedurally generated strangers, but with their trusted, battle-hardened veterans setting out on a new adventure. The reset becomes a highly desirable opportunity to experiment with new class combinations and relic builds for familiar faces, rather than an irreversible, punishing loss of progress. No character is ever retired or lost without the explicit, clear warning and manual approval of the player.The First-Season Arc StructureThe first season must operate as a macro-tutorial, slowly unfolding the complexities of the simulation over several hours before culminating in the first reset. A static dashboard populated with dropdown menus and raw data streams will instantly induce the dreaded "spreadsheet" feeling, alienating the target demographic.The Living Guild Hall vs. The DashboardThe main interface must be a spatially coherent, diorama-style representation of the guild hall. Characters should physically path around the environment, autonomously interacting with the facilities the player has constructed. If Ren is severely wounded following an expedition, his sprite should be visibly seen resting in a bed in the Tavern or sitting quietly at a table adorned with bandages. If two characters have formed a synergy bond during combat, they should occasionally be seen conversing near the hearth. This ambient, unscripted behavior requires absolutely no input from the player, but it constantly and subtly reinforces the narrative that these are living, breathing entities with interior lives, not just data points waiting for optimization.News Batching vs. The Pop-Up PlagueA severe and widely criticized flaw in mobile-first management games, particularly those from developers like Kairosoft, is the incessant, aggressive use of modal pop-ups. These notifications constantly pause the game to deliver trivial information, completely shattering immersion and frustrating the player to the point of abandonment.The proposed simulation must implement a strict philosophy of "News Batching." Minor, routine events—such as "The blacksmith crafted a new iron ingot," or "A local merchant is offering a minor discount"—should appear as a non-intrusive, continuous scrolling ticker at the bottom of the screen, or as a subtle notification dot on a "Ledger" icon. The game should only ever interrupt the player with a modal pop-up and demand immediate attention for crucial, actionable decisions: a hero is on the verge of permanent injury, a major boss expedition is ready to launch, or an exceptionally rare recruit has appeared at the gates.The "Return Report" following an expedition serves as the primary, satisfying batching mechanism. Rather than interrupting the battle to show every single item picked up, the report summarizes all loot, gold, experience points, and memory generation on a single, highly polished, satisfying screen.Accessibility and Adaptive User InterfaceTo accommodate a diverse range of hardware platforms—from ultrawide desktop monitors to vertical mobile phone screens—and to support players with varying needs, the user interface must be designed adaptively from the ground up. Utilizing established principles of adaptive UI architecture, which separates the visual style definitions from the underlying content model via a dynamic render tree, ensures that horizontal UI elements will naturally and cleanly wrap to new rows on smaller screens without breaking functionality or requiring extensive, platform-specific code rewrites.Furthermore, accessibility considerations must be foundational, not retrofitted. The auto-battle sequences, while designed to be readable, must feature a prominent "Reduced Motion" toggle. This toggle will limit screen shake, disable rapid particle flashes, and smooth out erratic animations, which are known to cause severe discomfort or distraction for players with vestibular sensitivities. Font sizes must be fully scalable to ensure legibility on small phone screens. Critically, the UI should never rely exclusively on "hover-states" (tooltips that only appear when a mouse cursor rests on an element) for conveying vital information, as this completely alienates mobile users relying on touch controls and players navigating via gamepads, erecting a hidden barrier to entry.First-Session Failure: Mitigation and RecoveryFailure during the first session is an inevitability for a subset of the player base. They may recruit entirely incompatible classes (e.g., a party composed entirely of support healers), spend all their initial gold on frivolous cosmetic upgrades while ignoring health recovery, or stubbornly send an exhausted party into a fatal encounter. If the simulation punishes this early experimentation too harshly by deleting their save or forcing a hard restart, the player will simply uninstall the application. The game must provide graceful, educational recovery paths while clearly explaining the underlying cause of the failure.Testing Comprehension, Not Just OutcomesTo ensure players actually understand the underlying mechanics—rather than just brute-forcing their way to victory via lucky critical hits—the early game should feature specific "Puzzle Encounters." These are highly curated, low-stakes enemy configurations designed to test a single, specific mechanic, drawing inspiration from the onboarding philosophies of successful roguelikes.For example, the game might present an enemy that is entirely immune to physical damage. The player is forced to either swap a magic-using class into their formation or equip an elemental damage relic to Ren. If they fail to recognize this requirement and send a purely physical party, the enemy will deal very little damage, allowing the party to eventually retreat safely or fail gracefully. The player is not punished severely, but the mechanical lesson—that damage types matter—is clearly and definitively established.The Diagnostic Defeat ScreenWhen a party is inevitably defeated, the resulting screen must not merely read "Expedition Failed" in red text. It must operate as a sophisticated diagnostic tool. Using readable metrics akin to synchronous auto-battlers, the game should explicitly inform the player of the specific breaking point that led to the collapse.For example, the screen might state: "Defeat! The enemy frontline possessed heavy physical armor, negating your primary attacks. Furthermore, your frontline collapsed due to a lack of sustained healing." This transforms a punitive loss into a highly educational moment. The player now knows exactly what to look for in their next recruitment phase (a Mage to bypass armor, or a Cleric to provide healing) or their next equipment purchase.The Safety Net MechanismThe simulation must implement an infallible anti-soft-lock mechanism. If the player experiences a total party wipe, has zero gold remaining, and possesses no items to sell, they must not be forced to abandon their save file. Drawing inspiration from Darkest Dungeon's ever-arriving Stagecoach, the guild hall should feature a "Desperate Mercenary" or "Wandering Volunteer" mechanic.If the roster falls below the minimum required to launch an expedition, a basic, level one unit will arrive at the guild and offer to join for free. They may not possess optimal stats, and they may lack the charm of the initial recruits, but they serve a vital mechanical purpose: they allow the player to run low-level, safe expeditions to inject gold back into the stagnant economy, allowing the recovery process to begin.Alternative Teaching ApproachesDepending on player reception and analytic data gathered during early access testing, different teaching paradigms can be employed to optimize the onboarding experience. The following three approaches represent viable alternatives to standard tutorialization.The Contextual Mentor (Recommended): An experienced, diagetic NPC (such as a retired Guildmaster or a seasoned quartermaster) provides advice only when a failure state is rapidly approaching. For example, if the player attempts to launch an expedition with a party whose average health is below thirty percent, the mentor intervenes with a polite, skippable warning. This preserves immersion by keeping the tutorial grounded in the world, while actively preventing fatal, frustrating errors before they occur.The "Holographic" Scout Simulation: Before the player commits to launching an expedition, they are given the option to run a "scout simulation." This plays out a hypothetical, accelerated auto-battle against the upcoming enemy formation. If the simulation results in a party wipe, the player can adjust their formation, swap relics, or recruit new members without any negative consequence. This completely eliminates trial-and-error frustration and heavily reinforces the puzzle-solving aspect of preparation, though it risks reducing the dramatic tension of the actual, finalized expedition.The Post-Mortem Analytics UI: The game allows the player to fail freely and without warning, but provides an incredibly detailed, interactive "Combat Log" after the fact. The player can scrub backward through a timeline of the battle, seeing exactly where every point of damage and healing originated. While this appeals heavily to hardcore min-maxers and theory-crafters, it risks violating the mandate to avoid a "spreadsheet-heavy" environment, potentially alienating the cozy demographic intended for the simulation.Ranked Priority Table for Vertical Slice ValidationThe following ten features are ranked by priority to strictly guide the development of the initial, approved vertical slice.
